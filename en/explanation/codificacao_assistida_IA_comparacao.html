Comparing AI Models for Qualitative Coding

A systematic evaluation of five LLMs generating Synesis annotations

AI-assisted qualitative coding depends critically on the language model used. Different models produce annotations with distinct levels of relational precision, thematic coverage and syntactic conformity. This document presents a comparative evaluation of five LLMs applied to the same scientific abstract, using a manually reviewed gold-standard annotation as reference.

Real data

All data presented here are real — extracted from a published scientific article and processed by synesis-coder with the same prompts and template. The reference annotation was manually reviewed by a researcher.

Record of a dated experiment

This evaluation dates from April 2026 and uses the models and tool versions available then. It rests on a single abstract and one run per model: it illustrates a comparison method and does not support generalizations about the models. In the current compiler, quotation marks in the text field do not cause a compilation error: they become part of the stored value, which is still undesirable.

1 The Test Case

The abstract used as the test case belongs to the article by Agaton et al. (2020) on socio-economic and environmental analyses of sustainable public transport in the Philippines.

1.1 Original Abstract (in full)

Electric vehicles are regarded as energy transition technology towards more sustainable and environment-friendly transportation systems. Despite the benefits of reducing the dependence on fossil fuels and greenhouse gas emissions, the adoption of electric vehicles faces several obstacles ranging from financing issues, government policies, and public acceptance. This study aims to identify the economic, environmental, and social impact of the adoption of electric vehicles for public transportation. Using the Philippines as a case study, the findings highlight the economic advantage of investing in electric public transportation with high public acceptance. The results further identify significant decrease in air pollution, reduction of greenhouse gas emissions and encourage lowering the reliance on imported fossil fuels by shifting the public transport from conventional to electric transport system. This study recommends stricter implementation of government policies on modernized public transportation, stronger government support on financing mechanisms, establishment of charging stations in public and private terminals, and boosting programs for developing local-made electric vehicles. To make electric vehicle more environment-friendly, the government must accelerate the energy transition by increasing the electricity share from renewable sources and investing in more sustainable sources of energy.

This abstract was chosen because it contains multiple causal mechanisms — barriers to adoption, enabling relations, environmental effects and policy recommendations — offering a rich test field for assessing each model’s analytical capability.

2 Methodological Questions

Before presenting the comparative results, it is necessary to put in context what “variation” means in qualitative coding — and why differences between models (or between human coders) do not necessarily invalidate the analysis.

2.1 Variation Is Not Error

Qualitative research and, consequently, the pursuit of objectivity and rigour involve a series of questions. First, the fact that there are different highlights and codings does not invalidate the effort of qualitative analysis. One of the main points, as we will see below, is the transparency and traceability of data. Just as all research — qualitative or quantitative — requires constant review cycles, AI-assisted qualitative coding is not exempt from the same approach.

Analysis of the items extracted by the models reveals a sophisticated effort to translate the text into chains of causal and logical relations, following the instructions in the prompt (GUIDELINES) to look for causal relations. However, although extraction captures the themes of the abstract, analytical precision varies. At several points the interpretation goes beyond the original text, forcing causal and sequential relations where the text presents only correlation or parallel lists.

2.2 The Problem of Ontological Granularity

Ideally, the codes (factors) assigned to the text would keep the same wording used in the original context. In an extensive corpus, however, this would produce a high degree of factor granularity, making analysis infeasible. Here topic modelling techniques are essential to group semantically close concepts under labels that preserve meaning without proliferating indefinitely.

In Synesis the template defines granularity control rules (Factor Granularity Control) that guide both the human researcher and the AI model on when to preserve a term’s specificity and when to generalize it. Even so, as the results show, each model draws that line differently.

2.3 Coding as an Interpretive Process

According to Saldaña (2009, p. 3), “a code in qualitative inquiry is most often a word or short phrase that symbolically assigns a summative, salient, essence-capturing, and/or evocative attribute for a portion of language-based or visual data.” It is in this sense that we use the term factor in Synesis.

Crucially, the coding process is directly influenced by each researcher’s analytical lens; the same data set can be coded in many different ways (Saldaña, 2009, pp. 7–8). This applies equally to AI models: each has its own interpretive biases affecting excerpt selection, concept naming and relation choice.

2.4 Factors as Fuzzy Concepts

Fuzzy set logic, introduced by Zadeh (1965), offers a theoretical basis for understanding why variation in coding is not arbitrary. According to Zadeh, the classes of objects encountered in the real physical world do not have precisely defined criteria of membership (Zadeh, 1965, p. 338). Unlike Boolean algebra (where an element either belongs to a set or not), in fuzzy logic the degree of membership is a continuum.

In qualitative coding, similar terms — such as bathing use, beach access, sea bathing — may be associated with the same factor (Bathing_Quality) with varying degrees of affinity without that being arbitrary. Ragin (2008), applying this logic to sociology, argues that qualitative analysis is fundamentally about establishing relations (Ragin, 2008, p. 2) between theoretical sets, and that attention to consistent connections is the cornerstone of the technique commonly known as analytic induction (Ragin, 2008, p. 2).

This is directly relevant to the present comparison: when two models give different names to the same concept (e.g. Financial_Barrier vs Financing), the question is not which name is “right”, but which better preserves the concept’s function within the project’s network of relations.

2.5 Rigour Criteria for AI-assisted Coding

Shenton (2004) sets out four criteria for grounding qualitative research, adapted from the traditional criteria of the natural sciences:

  • Credibility (instead of validity): how congruent are the findings with reality? In AI-assisted coding this shows in the traceability of each chain to the source text — none of the models tested hallucinated, which is a positive indicator of credibility.
  • Transferability (instead of generalization): are the results applicable to other contexts? Comparison across multiple models shows that the structural findings (barriers, enablers, environmental effects) are consistent across models, even if granularity varies.
  • Dependability (instead of reproducibility): is the process explicit enough to be replicated? Standardized prompts, declarative templates and documented command lines support procedural dependability.
  • Confirmability (instead of objectivity): do the findings reflect the data and not the researcher’s bias? In Shenton’s words, the beliefs underpinning decisions made and methods adopted should be acknowledged within the research report (Shenton, 2004, p. 72). In the AI context this translates into the audit trail — the record of each interpretive decision, which in Synesis is materialized by the note field of each ITEM.

2.6 The Audit Trail as a Differentiator

The audit trail is central to giving coding its rigour:

Qualitative researchers use the audit trail to establish a study’s rigour by providing the details of data analysis and some of the decisions that led to the findings. […] This record provides evidence that the raw data went through a process of analysis, reduction and synthesis. (Wolf, 2003, p. 175)

In Synesis the audit trail operates at several levels: in the note field of each ITEM (justifying the interpretation), in factor memos (documenting merges and splits) and in the ontology (recording the modal classification of each concept). This means that even when two models produce different annotations, both are auditable — the researcher can trace each decision and evaluate it critically.

2.7 Technical Limitations

AI-assisted coding applies advanced LLMs that still need calibration for specific domains. A future strategy might involve building corpora extensively reviewed by competent researchers and using them to calibrate models, yielding significant gains in hermeneutic accuracy. It is therefore necessary to acknowledge the quality achieved while seeking constant improvement.

Implication for reading the results

Quantitative differences between models (number of ITEMs, chain coverage) should be read in light of this discussion: more ITEMs does not necessarily mean better analysis, just as fewer chains does not necessarily mean worse coverage. The decisive criterion is interpretive quality — the ability to identify correct causal mechanisms, select appropriate relations and produce analytical notes that add value for the researcher.

3 The Reference Annotation

The gold-standard annotation (Padrão/agaton2020.syn) was manually reviewed and contains 12 ITEMs with 13 causal chains, organized into five thematic axes.

Why 12 ITEMs?

The reference annotation strictly follows the template GUIDELINES: parallel factors that independently converge on the same outcome are decomposed into separate ITEMs, each with its own note describing the individual mechanism. This reflects the principle of parallel vs sequential chains defined in the template.

8 Qualitative Observations

8.1 Relation Selection

The most important differentiator between models is the choice of relation (CONSTRAINS vs ENABLES vs INFLUENCES vs RELATES-TO). The reference uses:

  • CONSTRAINS for the three barrier factors (financing, policy, public acceptance)
  • RELATES-TO for the economic benefit ↔︎ social acceptance association (generic, non-causal association)
  • INFLUENCES for the effects of electrification on the environment
  • ENABLES for the enablers and the energy chain

Opus reproduced this selection exactly. The other models made polarity errors:

Model Error Impact
Haiku Public_Acceptance -> INFLUENCES (should be CONSTRAINS) Loses the barrier semantics
Sonnet Economic_Benefit -> INFLUENCES (reference uses RELATES-TO) Imposes causality where there is association
Gemma4 Public_Acceptance -> ENABLES (should be CONSTRAINS) Reverses the meaning of the source text
Qwen Public_Acceptance -> ENABLES -> Investment Captures a different mechanism (valid, but not matching the reference)

8.2 Parallel Decomposition vs Compression

The reference decomposes parallel factors into separate ITEMs — three ITEMs for the barriers (Axis 1) and four for the enablers (Axis 4). This follows the template GUIDELINES, which instruct:

PARALLEL (X,Y,Z→A): Create SEPARATE ITEM blocks when multiple factors independently affect the same outcome.

  • Opus: complete decomposition (12 ITEMs) — identical to the reference
  • Qwen: partial decomposition — decomposed barriers (3 ITEMs) but not enablers
  • Gemma4: partial decomposition — decomposed barriers but with a relational error
  • Haiku and Sonnet: compressed everything into 5 ITEMs — not decomposed

8.3 Format and Encoding

Two models produced output with format problems:

  • Sonnet: added double quotes ("...") to the text field, which alters the value stored by the compiler. It also showed corrupted em-dash characters (ÔÇö instead of ---).
  • Gemma4: added single quotes ('...') to the text field, inconsistent with Synesis convention.
UTF-16 encoding

All output files from the Anthropic models were encoded as UTF-16LE with BOM. This is an artefact of PowerShell redirection (>>), not of the model itself.

10 Conclusions

  1. Opus 4.6 is the only model that reproduced the analytical strategy of the human reference. The parallel decomposition, correct relation selection and use of *complex*/*borderline* flags show a deep grasp of the template GUIDELINES.

  2. No model hallucinated. All five models produced annotations traceable to the source text — a positive indicator of the reliability of the synesis-coder prompts.

  3. CONSTRAINS vs ENABLES is the quality differentiator. Models that got the polarity of the relation wrong (Haiku: INFLUENCES; Gemma4: ENABLES) show a shallow understanding of the barrier context described in the text.

  4. Smaller models compress instead of decomposing. The tendency to generate 5–6 ITEMs (instead of the reference’s 12) indicates that smaller models favour synthesis over analytical decomposition — the opposite of what the GUIDELINES instruct for parallel factors.

  5. Format is a knock-out criterion. Quotes in the text field (Sonnet, Gemma4) alter the stored value and hinder literal checking against the source. Only Opus, Haiku and Qwen produced output usable without format adjustment.

10.1 Practical Recommendations

  • For production (final corpus): Claude Opus 4.6 — superior relational precision and structural coverage.
  • For quick drafting / triage: Claude Haiku 4.5 — clean format, high speed, low cost; requires human review of relations and decomposition.
  • For environments without a commercial API: Qwen 3.5:397b — best open-weight model tested, with good barrier decomposition; requires review of the enabler axes.
